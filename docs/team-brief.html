01 · Cause

Land records are flat. Cities are not.

The whole project follows from one sentence, and everyone on the team should be able to say it cold.

Today a land parcel is identified by where it sits on the ground. That is a polygon: a boundary drawn on a map, with an owner attached. The system works perfectly for a field. It stopped working the moment somebody built a first floor.

In any Indian city block, one set of ground coordinates now carries a dozen or more legally distinct things, owned by different people, governed by different authorities:

What that costs, in practice

  • Apartment ownership is recorded on paper, not in the cadastre. A flat is a share in a society plus a sale deed. There is no spatial record of which cubic metres you bought, so boundary disputes between neighbouring flats have no authoritative geometry to resolve them against.
  • Utilities get cut. A builder excavating a basement has no reliable machine-readable record of what runs beneath. Every city has a steady rate of struck water mains, cables and gas lines.
  • Unauthorised construction is invisible to the file. If a builder adds a floor beyond sanction, the paper record still agrees with itself perfectly. Nothing in the record can contradict it.
  • Air rights and TDR have no spatial identity. Development rights are traded, but the volume being traded cannot be pointed at.
  • Metro, utility and property records live in different systems with no shared identifier, so "is this foundation clear of the tunnel?" is a meeting, not a query.

The one-line version

A 2D identifier can name the column. It cannot name anything inside it. We are giving every legally meaningful volume of urban space its own identity.

02 · Solution

What we actually built

Not a 3D map. A land record that happens to be three-dimensional.

Bhoomi3D takes the data a survey already produces — drone LiDAR, an orthophoto, GNSS ground control, the existing 2D parcel layer, approved building plans, utility records — and produces a validated register in which every flat, shop, parking bay, basement, tunnel segment and block of air rights is a solid with a unique number.

Seven things it does, in order:

  1. Ingests real survey formats — LAS/LAZ, GeoTIFF, GeoJSON — not a bespoke format.
  2. Extracts buildings automatically from the point cloud. Nobody draws a polygon.
  3. Finds the floors inside each building from the facade, without entering it.
  4. Builds 3D parcels — a footprint swept between two elevations, one per legal object.
  5. Issues a 3D-ULPIN to each, with a check character that catches typing errors offline.
  6. Validates the whole register: overlapping ownership, unclaimed space, clearance breaches, unauthorised storeys.
  7. Serves it through a REST API and a 3D viewer that runs with no internet connection.

The distinction to insist on

A viewer shows you a building. A cadastre can answer "who owns the cubic metres at this coordinate and elevation, and what is the evidence?" Every object we store carries an identifier, an owner, a parent, and a record of whether its boundary was surveyed, taken from an approved plan, or inferred by our software. That last field is what makes it a record rather than a picture.

03 · Method

How it works — the eight stages

Learn the why column. Judges ask "why that algorithm", not "what does it do".

01
GNSS / CORS datum adjustment
A drone block is internally consistent but floats in an arbitrary datum. A Helmert (7-parameter) fit ties it to the national frame. Bad ground control is common, so we use Baarda data snooping — testing each residual against the known GNSS precision, not against its own scatter, because one blunder inflates that scatter enough to hide itself.
0.04 s
02
Ground filtering — SMRF
Separates bare earth from everything standing on it, producing the terrain model every later height is measured from. Published algorithm (Pingel et al., ISPRS 2013). Chosen because it has exactly two parameters with physical meaning — largest object to remove, steepest plausible terrain — that a surveyor can actually tune.
0.75 s
03
Building extraction
Eigenvalue features separate flat roofs from scattered tree canopy; DBSCAN splits the result into individual buildings; an alpha shape traces each outline; regularisation squares it to its dominant wall direction. Trees are the dominant false positive in every automated cadastral pipeline, and this is the stage that removes them.
11.5 s
04
Imagery cross-check
The orthophoto is extracted independently and matched against the LiDAR result. Two sensors agreeing is evidence; one sensor repeating itself is not. Anything found by only one sensor gets flagged for a surveyor.
3.2 s
05
Import the existing 2D record
The legacy cadastral layer is imported as-is and given a vertical extent. We extend the existing record; we do not replace it. Its horizontal boundaries keep their original authority.
< 0.01 s
06
Storey segmentation
The one that sounds impossible. Building facades repeat vertically — balcony slabs, window bands, string courses all at the storey pitch — so the facade's height histogram carries a periodic signal whose period is the floor-to-floor height. We recover it by autocorrelation and the phase by matched filtering. Gets 3.85 m commercial floors right, which a fixed 3 m assumption does not.
1.0 s
07
Volumetric delineation & ULPIN minting
Builds the parcel → building → storey → unit hierarchy, plus air rights above and infrastructure corridors below, and mints an identifier for each. Where an approved floor plan exists it is used verbatim; where none exists the storey is registered as one unallocated volume rather than inventing flat boundaries.
0.06 s
08
Validation — ten rules
Overlapping ownership volumes, unclaimed floor area, containment breaches, storey gaps, infrastructure clearance, unauthorised storeys, area mismatches, duplicate identifiers, degenerate geometry, and a list of everything still awaiting ground verification.
0.07 s

The technology, and why each choice

LayerWhat we useWhy this and not the obvious alternative
Geometry kernel Prisms + Shapely Nearly every legal solid is a footprint swept between two elevations. Restricting the primitive to a prism makes overlap volumes and clearances exact and closed-form — no mesh boolean library, no tolerance tuning.
Coordinates Local ENU on WGS-84 Metric, so areas and volumes are directly meaningful. Full ellipsoid maths, no PROJ dependency — installs on a locked-down government machine without a GDAL toolchain.
Point-cloud AI SMRF, DBSCAN, RANSAC, PCA features Published, auditable algorithms that need no labelled training data. An Indian urban cadastral training set at the required quality does not exist; a model we could not explain would not be admissible anyway.
Imagery Spectral rejector + height fusion Deliberately honest: RGB alone cannot tell a roof from a paved forecourt, and our module demonstrates that failure before resolving it with height. A trained model drops into the same interface.
Backend Python, FastAPI, NumPy, SciPy Standard, reviewable, no exotic dependencies. The API is OpenAPI-documented automatically.
Viewer Hand-written WebGL2 No Three.js, no CDN, no network at runtime. A demo that fails because the venue wifi is down is a demo we lose.
Storage JSON document Correct at demonstration scale. The register class is the seam where PostGIS goes for a city-scale deployment — we say this out loud rather than being caught by it.
Every row here is a question a technical judge may open with.
04 · Identifier

The 3D-ULPIN

If we only get to explain one thing in detail, it is this.

27 - 025 - 004 - tek92et7bx - B04 - 00DY - 5
│     │     │         │        │ │    │     │
│     │     │         │        │ │    │     └─ check character (ISO 7064 MOD 37,36)
│     │     │         │        │ │    └─────── unit sequence on that level
│     │     │         │        │ └──────────── level index
│     │     │         │        └────────────── stratum: G B U A I
│     │     │         └─────────────────────── geohash-10 of the centroid (1.1 m × 0.6 m)
│     │     └───────────────────────────────── tehsil / ULB code
│     └─────────────────────────────────────── district code (LGD)
└───────────────────────────────────────────── state code (LGD)
PropertyHow it is achieved
Unique in 3DHorizontal identity from the geohash, vertical identity from stratum + level. A flat on floor 10 and the metro tunnel beneath it share a geohash and differ in stratum.
Self-describingState, district, tehsil, above/below ground, which level, and the approximate position — all readable with no database lookup.
Error-detectingThe check character catches 100 % of single-character typos and 100 % of adjacent transpositions. We verify this exhaustively in tests — all 910 possible substitutions, not a sample.
StableMinted once, never changes. Re-survey drift is reported as an advisory against the cell recorded at mint time, never as a new number. Otherwise a 3 cm boundary correction could orphan every deed referencing the property.
Collision-resistantAdministrative namespacing, deterministic minting, and a registry constraint that refuses to re-issue a number to a different object — which is what catches conflicts when two offline registers merge.

The tension worth naming before a judge finds it

The geohash makes the number self-locating, but it is derived from the centroid — and re-surveys move centroids. We resolved that deliberately: the identifier is minted once and immutable, the geohash records where the object was at mint time, and drift is detected and reported rather than silently changing anyone's property number. Say this before they ask; it shows the design was thought through.

The line to use

"Four of the seven inputs are things a state land-records department, a municipal corporation and a metro authority already hold today. The only genuinely new acquisition is the drone flight — and under SVAMITVA, drone survey of inhabited areas is already national policy."

06 · Operation

Running it, and the demo script

Rehearse this until it takes four minutes with no dead air.

Setup, once

# create the environment and install dependencies
python -m venv .venv
.venv/Scripts/python -m pip install -r backend/requirements.txt

# build the demonstration dataset and run the whole pipeline (~30 s)
.venv/Scripts/python scripts/build_demo.py

# start the API and viewer
.venv/Scripts/python scripts/serve.py

Then open http://127.0.0.1:8000/. On macOS or Linux use .venv/bin/python. There is also a one-shot scripts/run.ps1 that does all three.

Before we present

Run build_demo.py and start the server before we walk in, and leave the browser open on the loaded viewer. Never build the dataset live — 30 seconds of silence is 30 seconds of lost attention, and we can re-run the pipeline on stage from inside the app anyway (step 6).

The six-step demo

  1. Open on the loaded register. Top bar reads 143 objects · 97 units · 30 storeys · 3 corridors · 143 ULPINs · z −17.0 to 68.2 m. Say: "Nothing about these buildings was given to the software. It was handed a point cloud."
  2. Click a building, then a floor, then a flat. The inspector shows the 3D-ULPIN decoded field by field, plan area, volume, height, z-range, owner, and a provenance badge. Point at the badge — "this says the boundary came from an approved plan, not from our guess."
  3. Turn on Column probe and click the ground. The vertical stack appears as a scale strip: air rights, ten floors and their flats, the building, the parcel, two basements, the storm drain. "Twenty-nine objects at one coordinate. A 2D record has one row for this."
  4. Open the Validation tab. Five errors, each with the measured quantity that makes it an error. Walk the three headline ones: unauthorised storey, overlapping flats, drain through a basement raft. Press show in 3D — the camera flies to the defect and outlines it.
  5. Open the Accuracy tab. Every stage scored against ground truth. "A pipeline that cannot state its own accuracy has no business producing a legal record."
  6. Press "Re-run AI pipeline". All eight stages execute live and stream back in about seventeen seconds. This is the moment that proves it is a working system and not a rendered mock-up. Always end on this.

If they want to see the API instead

# everything in the vertical column at one position
GET /api/column?x=52&y=52

# resolve an identifier; check character verified before any lookup
GET /api/ulpin/27-025-004-tek92et7bx-B04-00DY-5

# what contains this exact 3D point
GET /api/at?lat=18.5209&lon=73.8572&z=31.5

# open the register in QGIS
GET /api/export/geojson

Interactive docs are at /docs — that is a strong thing to show a technical judge, because it is generated from the code, not written for the demo.

07 · Value

Where the time actually goes

Be precise about what we measured and honest about what we estimated. Judges punish invented numbers harder than modest ones.

Our machine-measured figure is exact: 17 seconds for a full city block of 609,177 points, producing 143 identified objects. Everything in the "manual" column below is an estimate of conventional practice, and we should present it as such.

The three time savings that matter most

  • Footprint digitisation disappears. This is the bulk of manual cadastral mapping effort, and it is fully automated at 98.4 % mean IoU against truth.
  • Errors are caught at data-entry time, not in court. An overlap between two flats found by a validator costs seconds. The same overlap found after both deeds are registered costs years of litigation. This is the real saving and it is not measured in machine seconds.
  • Clearance questions become queries. "Is this excavation clear of the tunnel?" changes from an inter-departmental letter to an API call returning a number.

Do not say this

Do not claim a specific multiplier — "100× faster", "saves 6 months per ward". We have not measured a manual baseline, and a judge who works in land records will know the real figure and will stop trusting everything else we said. Say "footprint digitisation is the largest manual cost in cadastral mapping and we automate it entirely; here is our measured accuracy."

08 · Defence

Questions judges will ask

Every answer below is grounded in something we can show on screen. If an answer is not demonstrable, do not give it.

Isn't this just a 3D visualisation? What makes it a cadastre?

A viewer draws a building. A cadastre answers who owns which cubic metres, with what evidence. Every object we store carries a unique identifier, an owner, a parent in a legal hierarchy, and a provenance field recording whether the boundary was surveyed, taken from an approved plan, or inferred by our software. We validate the whole register against ten topology rules and export to GeoJSON and CityGML.

Show: click a flat, point at the provenance badge, then open the Validation tab.

Where is the AI? These look like classical algorithms.

They are, and that is a deliberate engineering decision we can defend. We use eigenvalue-based semantic classification, density-based instance segmentation, RANSAC plane fitting and spectral analysis — the standard machine-learning toolkit for point clouds, from the ISPRS literature.

Three reasons we did not use deep learning here:

  • No labelled Indian urban cadastral dataset exists at the required quality. A model trained on Vaihingen or ISPRS Potsdam would not transfer.
  • The output has legal consequences. A surveyor has to be able to audit why a boundary was drawn where it was; "the network said so" is not admissible.
  • It works — F1 of 1.00 and mean IoU of 0.984 against ground truth. Adding an unexplainable model would not improve that.

We built the interface a trained model plugs into and left it documented and deliberately unimplemented rather than faking it.

Show: the Accuracy tab, and ai/image_segment.py — the OnnxBackend class.

Your data is synthetic. Does this work on real data?

This is the fairest question we will get, and we answer it directly. The data is synthetic so that ground truth exists — it is the only way to state accuracy as a number rather than an opinion. We know the true footprint, height and floor count of every building, so every stage is scored, not admired.

The simulator deliberately models what makes real extraction hard: trees taller than the low-rise buildings, parapets that make roofs read tall, occlusion that thins the lower storeys, sensor noise, and an unknown datum shift with a planted blunder in the ground control.

The pipeline reads real LAS/LAZ and GeoTIFF today. Point it at a real survey and it runs — the simulator is not load-bearing. What we would gain from real data is calibration, and that is our stated next step.

Never say: "it will definitely work the same on real data." Say: "these numbers prove the algorithms are correct; real data proves they are calibrated, and that is the next step."

How does this scale from one block to a whole city?

Honestly: the algorithms scale, the storage layer does not yet. Every extraction stage is tiled and near-linear — validation uses a sort-and-sweep spatial index rather than comparing every pair. The register is currently a JSON document, which is correct for hundreds of objects and wrong for millions.

We isolated that deliberately: the register class is a single interface that a PostGIS-backed store replaces without touching any other stage. That is a known, scoped piece of work, not an unknown.

Strength to convert: naming your own limitation precisely, with the fix already scoped, reads as engineering maturity. Being caught out on it reads as the opposite.

Doesn't ArcGIS / Bentley / CityEngine already do this?

They do 3D city modelling. They do not do 3D cadastre. The difference is identity and legal validation: none of them mints a collision-resistant, check-summed property identifier for a volumetric parcel, or validates a register for overlapping ownership and statutory clearance breaches.

They are also proprietary and expensive per seat. Ours has no licence cost, no external service dependency, and runs offline on a laptop — which matters a great deal for a tehsil office.

Is there a legal basis for a 3D ULPIN? Can a state actually use this?

Be careful and truthful here. ULPIN exists under DILRMP; a volumetric extension of it does not yet have a statutory framework, and we should not claim otherwise.

What we can say: apartment ownership already has legal standing under state apartment-ownership acts and RERA, and the identifier we designed is deliberately built as an extension of the existing LGD-coded ULPIN rather than a replacement — the first three fields are the existing administrative codes. The 2D record we import keeps its original authority. Adoption is a policy decision; we built the technical instrument it would need.

Say: "we are not asking anyone to replace the land record. We extend it, and everything we infer is flagged as inferred until a surveyor signs it off."

What if the drone survey is poor quality — occlusion, dense trees, narrow lanes?

Degradation is graceful and visible rather than silent. Confidence is computed per building from three independent signals — how building-like the points were, how completely the footprint was covered, and how far regularisation had to move the outline. Low-confidence objects are flagged in the validation report for ground verification and are prioritised in that list.

The storey stage behaves the same way: if the facade shows no periodic signal it reports a low periodicity strength and falls back to uniform division, and the validator downgrades an unauthorised-storey finding from an error to a warning because an enforcement action needs evidence.

Show: the "scan confidence" figure inside the unauthorised-storey finding.

Who is the user? A tehsildar is not going to run a Python script.

Three distinct users, and only one of them touches the pipeline:

  • The survey contractor or state GIS cell runs the pipeline once per area — that is a specialist, technical job.
  • The land-records officer uses the viewer and the search box: type a ULPIN or an owner name, inspect the property, read the validation findings.
  • Other departments use the API — the metro authority querying clearance, the building-permission cell querying what exists on a plot.

The viewer needs no training beyond "click the thing you want to know about".

What does it cost to deploy?

No licence cost — every dependency is open source. No external service, no API keys, no cloud requirement; it runs on a laptop and works offline. The real cost is the drone survey, which under SVAMITVA is already being funded and flown for inhabited areas.

Do not quote a rupee figure per ward. We have not costed one.

How do you handle privacy? This is ownership data.

The platform stores what a land record already stores and adds no new personal data — no Aadhaar, no phone numbers, no financial information. It runs entirely on-premise with no outbound network calls, so data never leaves the department's machine. Access control would be inherited from the department's existing land-records system rather than reinvented by us.

What is genuinely novel here? Surely someone has done 3D cadastre.

3D cadastre is an established research field — we should say so rather than pretend otherwise; it has an FIG working group and pilots in the Netherlands, Singapore and Queensland. What we have not found combined in one working system:

  • A volumetric identifier designed as a backward-compatible extension of India's existing ULPIN, with provable offline error detection.
  • Fully automated extraction from raw survey to identified volumes, with no manual digitisation step.
  • Validation that spans property and infrastructure together — flats and metro tunnels checked against each other by the same rule engine.
  • Self-scoring: the pipeline reports its own measured accuracy on every run.
Show me it is not hard-coded to your demo.

Press Re-run AI pipeline. Every stage executes live from the raw point cloud and streams its result back. Then open the Accuracy tab — the numbers are recomputed, not stored strings.

For a technical judge: python -m pytest -q runs 124 tests in about ten seconds, including an end-to-end test that runs the real pipeline and asserts the accuracy thresholds.

This is our strongest single move. Keep a terminal open on a second window with the test command ready.

09 · Risk register

Where we can lose

Ranked by how likely it is to cost us. Read this section twice — knowing our weak points is worth more than rehearsing our strong ones.

1 · "It only works on data you made up" Highest risk

Why it bites It is true, it is obvious, and if we look defensive the entire accuracy table stops counting.

How we answer Lead with it before they raise it. "Synthetic so that ground truth exists — otherwise we could not put a number on accuracy at all." Then show that the simulator models the hard cases deliberately: trees taller than buildings, parapets, occlusion, a planted control blunder.

What would kill this objection A single real LAS tile from any Indian city run through the pipeline, even without ground truth — just to show it ingests and produces plausible footprints. If we can get one before the finals, do it. This is the highest-value remaining task on the project.

2 · "This isn't AI/ML, it's image processing" High risk

Why it bites The problem statement asks for AI/ML. A judge scanning for a neural network will not find one, and some judges score on buzzwords.

How we answer Name the methods precisely — unsupervised clustering, PCA-based feature extraction, RANSAC model fitting, spectral analysis — and cite the ISPRS literature they come from. Then give the three reasons (no dataset, auditability, it already works) and point at the ONNX interface we built for a trained model.

Tone Confident, not apologetic. "We chose interpretable models because the output has legal consequences" is a stronger position than a black box — but only if we say it like we mean it.

3 · No government stakeholder has validated it Medium-high

Why it bites SIH judges weight deployability heavily. "Have you spoken to a land records department?" is a very common question and we currently have to say no.

How we answer Do not bluff. Say what we grounded the design in instead: LGD administrative codes, the existing ULPIN structure, DILRMP, SVAMITVA drone survey, and real municipal clearance rules for utility corridors. Then state the specific next step — a pilot on one ward with one ULB.

Before finals Even one 20-minute conversation with a municipal GIS or land-records officer, quoted by name and designation, converts this from a weakness into a strength.

4 · Scale is untested Medium

Why it bites One block is not a city, and JSON storage is an easy thing to attack.

How we answer Concede the storage layer immediately and precisely, and note that the extraction stages are already tiled and near-linear. Say where PostGIS goes and why nothing else changes when it does.

Cheap mitigation Run the pipeline on a larger simulated extent and quote the timing. This is a few hours of work and turns a soft answer into a measured one.

5 · The legal framework for a 3D ULPIN does not exist Medium

Why it bites A policy-minded judge can argue the whole thing is premature.

How we answer Agree, then reframe. Policy follows capability: the reason there is no framework is partly that there has been no instrument. Stress that we extend rather than replace — the first three fields are the existing administrative codes, the imported 2D boundaries keep their authority, and everything we infer is flagged as inferred until signed off.

6 · The demo fails live Medium · entirely preventable

Why it bites It ends the presentation regardless of how good the work is.

Prevention checklist

  • Dataset built and server started before entering the room.
  • Browser already open on the loaded viewer, not on a blank tab.
  • The viewer needs no internet — but confirm the machine is not on a proxy that intercepts localhost.
  • Second machine with the identical state, ready to swap.
  • Screen-recorded run of the full demo as a fallback video.
  • Check the laptop supports WebGL2 — the viewer states this clearly if not, but find out beforehand.
7 · "Where is the mobile app / field verification?" Lower

How we answer We built the API first, deliberately — a field app is a client of it, and the validation report already produces a prioritised list of exactly which objects need ground verification. That list is the work order a field app would consume. Scoped, not overlooked.

8 · Someone on the team cannot explain their own module Silent killer

Why it bites Judges often direct a question at whoever has not spoken. One member saying "I'm not sure, that's his part" undoes the credibility of everything already said.

Fix Section 11 assigns an owner to every part. Everyone must be able to give a 30-second answer on any section, and a two-minute answer on their own. Rehearse by having each person answer a question from someone else's area.
